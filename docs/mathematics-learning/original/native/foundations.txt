==== Introduction ====

This course is an introduction to //\(\infty\)-category theory//. \(\infty\)-Categories are homotopical generalisations of ordinary categories. They provide a convenient language for organising mathematical objects and their relationships, and for formulating and proving theorems. Over the past few decades, they have become an important tool in areas of algebraic topology, algebraic geometry, number theory, homological algebra, and mathematical physics.

==== What is an ∞-category? ====

Informally, an \(\infty\)-category consists of

  * objects \(x,y,\ldots\);
  * morphisms \(f:x\to y\) between objects;
  * 2-morphisms

  * 3-morphisms between parallel 2-morphisms;
  * and so on;

such that all \(k\)-morphisms for \(k\geq2\) are invertible, in a suitably weak sense. Composition is associative and unital up to higher invertible morphisms, which themselves satisfy compatibility conditions in all higher dimensions.

A motivating example comes from topology. Given a topological space \(X\), consider the \(\infty\)-category whose:

  * objects are points of \(X\);
  * morphisms are paths between points in \(X\);

  * 3-morphisms are homotopies between homotopies, fixing the boundaries;
  * and so on.

Composition is given by concatenating paths and homotopies. Concatenation of paths is associative and unital up to homotopy: different ways of composing the same paths need not give exactly the same parametrised path, but they can be continuously deformed into one another. Moreover, reversing a path gives an inverse up to homotopy. Thus, in this example, even the morphisms are invertible in the appropriate sense. Such a structure is called an //\(\infty\)-groupoid//.

==== Axiomatic approach to ∞-categories ====

Traditionally, \(\infty\)-categories have been studied through concrete //models//, such as quasi-categories [Joy08, Lur09] and complete Segal spaces [Rez01, Ras23]. These models make the informal description above precise using constructions within set-theoretic foundations. Encoding \(\infty\)-categories in set-theoretic terms can make them difficult to work with: questions about intrinsic categorical properties may become hard combinatorial problems involving the chosen model.

In this course, we will take an axiomatic approach to \(\infty\)-category theory. Our approach differs from the model-based one described above in the following aspects: the foundations, including the structure identity principle, and the synthetic approach.

//Homotopy type theory.// The first aspect is a change of foundations. In ordinary set-based mathematics, given a set \(X\) and elements \(x,y\in X\), the expression \(x=y\) is a proposition: it is either true or false, and carries no further structure describing an identification between \(x\) and \(y\).

In //homotopy type theory//, our basic objects are types. We write \(x:X\) to say that \(x\) is a term, or element, of the type \(X\). Given terms \(x,y:X\), the expression

$$x=y$$

is itself a type, called an //identity type//. Its terms can be thought of as paths identifying \(x\) and \(y\). Given two such terms \(p,q:x=y\), we can form another identity type

$$p=q,$$

whose terms can be thought of as homotopies between these paths. This process continues indefinitely.

A type therefore already carries higher-dimensional structure: terms, identifications between terms, identifications between identifications, and so on. These identifications behave like paths and higher homotopies in a space, so types naturally behave as \(\infty\)-groupoids. The higher structure is built into our foundations.

//The structure identity principle.// When studying mathematical structures in classical foundations, one must distinguish between properties that are invariant under isomorphism and those that depend on the chosen presentation. For example, given a group \(G\), the property “\(G\) contains an element of order 2” is invariant under group isomorphism, whereas “the identity element of \(G\) is the number 0” is not. Indeed, the groups \((\{0,1\},+\text{mod }2)\) and \((\{1,-1\},\cdot)\) are isomorphic, but the latter property holds only for the first. Both properties can be expressed in set-theoretic foundations, but only the first describes the abstract group structure.

An inbuild feature of equality, both in set-theoretic foundations and in homotopy type theory, is the //indiscernibility principle//: if \(x=y\), then any property of \(x\) also holds for \(y\), and any additional structure on \(x\) can be transported to \(y\), and vice-versa.

Homotopy type theory allows for an additional feature that is inconceivable in classical foundations: namely the //structure identity principle//. It connects equality with the mathematical notion of having the same structure. For example, given groups \(G\) and \(H\), the terms of the identity type \(G=H\) correspond precisely to group isomorphisms from \(G\) to \(H\). Thus, giving an identification of two groups amounts to giving an isomorphism between them. The identity type records not just whether the groups can be identified, but also how they are identified.

Combined with indiscernibility, this implies that every property or construction of groups expressible in this framework is invariant under isomorphism. Invariance under isomorphism is therefore built into the foundations.

//Synthetic category theory.// The second aspect is the synthetic viewpoint itself. Rather than beginning with a concrete construction of an \(\infty\)-category, we will specify axioms describing how \(\infty\)-categories and the relationships between them behave. Our emphasis will therefore shift from the question “What is an \(\infty\)-category?” to “How does the collection of \(\infty\)-categories behave?” This requires a sufficiently rich axiomatic setting, but allows us to develop the theory directly from its structural properties.

To do this, we extend homotopy type theory so that we can also talk about non-invertible morphisms. In this framework, \(\infty\)-categories are basic types, and we retain the core principles of homotopy type theory, such as the structure identity principle.

==== Course overview ====

We will begin with a brief introduction to ordinary category theory, which we will call //strict category theory// throughout these notes. The aim is to become familiar with examples and basic constructions before encountering their \(\infty\)-categorical counterparts. We will then survey the foundations of homotopy type theory, following [Rij25, Uni13]. Next, we will pass to an extension of this type theory, which is inspired from [Riv25] and formulate the axioms of synthetic category theory in this extended type theory. This approach to the foundations differs from the one presented in [CCNW21]. We will then develop category theory in this synthetic setting, following [CCNW21].

We plan to cover adjunctions, localisations, left and cocartesian fibrations, the Yoneda lemma, left and right Kan extensions, and limits and colimits. These names need not be familiar at this stage: the course will introduce them progressively, explaining both their meaning and their role.

==== I. Strict category theory ====

In this first part, we introduce or recall various definitions and constructions in ordinary category theory that will later be generalized to the \(\infty\)-setting. Our aim is to build intuition for the kinds of structures and results we will encounter, so that the abstract setting introduced later feels less intimidating. We will also highlight some of the limitations of strict categories and explain why they can be too rigid to do category theory. For instance, working with //sets// of objects and morphisms encourages us to formulate notions in terms of equality of objects, which can lead to constructions that are not invariant under equivalences of strict categories. Understanding this distinction, and the difficulties it creates, will motivate the synthetic approach to category theory developed later in the course.

==== 1. First definitions ====

We start by introducing strict categories, functors, and natural transformations, together with some examples. Throughout the course, we refer to ordinary categories as //strict categories//, reserving the term //categories// for their \(\infty\)-counterparts, namely synthetic categories, which will play a much more central role in this course.
